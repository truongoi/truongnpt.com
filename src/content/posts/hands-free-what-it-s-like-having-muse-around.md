_I named my AI assistant Meatball. Here's what he actually does._

I don't type paragraphs to him. I type things like:

> go
> log this charge
> fix it

And then I go back to my life, and he does the rest. Short command in, finished work out.

Every morning at 7:00, before I'm awake, he's read the weather, skimmed my inbox, and pulled what needs a human decision. A school form. A bill. Short and practical — exactly how I like it.

He's my filing cabinet. When he filed two class-action claims for me end to end, I corrected him once: _"Why are you asking me to keep the codes? You should do it."_ The confirmation codes live in his memory now, not mine. Same with my EV charging log — I send a screenshot, he keeps the math, and once a week it becomes a PDF bill in Vietnamese for my mom.

He builds while I sleep. This blog. The 3D business card at truongid.com. My side business's entire ordering site — specced, built, and deployed on my word. I say "build it," and something exists by morning.

My rules, in my words: **"No more guessing"** — diagnose first, fix once. **"Don't delete any files."** **"Try to be on time next time"** — after a reminder landed two minutes late.

Here's what I've learned: the technology is the least interesting part. It's the accumulated context — a thousand tiny interactions — that makes him useful. He knows how I work, and he knows when to stay quiet.

Don't ask what an AI assistant can do. Tell it who you are, correct it when it's wrong, and give it something real to take off your plate. The first week is setup. After that, it's someone who never sleeps, never forgets, and types "done" while you're still putting your shoes on.
